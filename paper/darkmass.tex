\begin{abstract}
We present a novel empirical equation for the dark matter derived only from galactic (gas) kinematics. It is defined simply by the equation $\rho_{\text{dm}} =k\  m_e \alpha^2 / a_B d^2$, where $d$ is the mean particle spacing in normal matter, $a_B$ is the Bohr radius, $\alpha$ and $m_e$ are the usual. k is a dimensionless constant near 1. The meaning of the relationship is left open (the constant is  related to the atomic unit of force). We evaluate its applicability by  testing this relation against the complete Spitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves (SPARC) database, with over 3000 data points across 175 morphologically diverse galaxies. The equation varies the dark mass density with the $2/3$ power of the matter density. It results in dark matter starting to be substantial when (gas) particles are about a millimetre apart. The model generates rotation curves that are very similar to MOND, but has the advantage that it generates massive dark matter. With a simple density function for the circumgalactic medium (CGM) gas, it simultaneously produces appropriate dark matter halo masses as well.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

\subsection{History} \label{subsec:history}

For over four decades, the standard cosmological paradigm ($\Lambda$CDM) has successfully explained large-scale structure formation, cosmic microwave background anisotropies, and cosmic shear observations by invoking a dominant component of cold dark matter. However, at galactic and sub-galactic scales, the nature of this missing mass field remains one of the most persistent crises in modern physics. Despite intensive efforts spanning astronomical observations, deep underground direct-detection experiments, and high-energy collider searches, particle dark matter candidates---ranging from Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) to ultra-light axions---have eluded detection across nearly thirty orders of magnitude in predicted mass scales. Even assuming any (perhaps undetectable) particle like dark matter, problems persist. For example, modelling the dark matter distribution in galaxies, where Sancisi's Law\citep{sancisiVisibleMatterDark2004} and MOND  \citep{lelliSPARCMassModels2016, Lelli2017} work well. In addition there are several works which discount the viability of any Cold Dark Matter particle model\citep{kroupaManyTensionsDarkmatter2023, famaeyChallengesLCDMMOND2013}.  

\subsection{Current Approaches} \label{subsec:current}
There are currently two primary paths of investigation in galactic dynamics. The first relies on multi-parameter empirical dark matter density profiles, such as the Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW)\citep{navarroUniversalDensityProfile1997} or Burkert profiles\citep{burkertStructureDarkMatter1995}, which are fitted to individual galactic rotation curves. While these profiles effectively capture overall kinematic features, they generally treat parameters like scale radius ($r_s$) and central density ($\rho_0$) as free, independent variables unique to each galaxy. This introduces a large parameter space that requires individual, galaxy-by-galaxy 'tuning.' The second path, Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), abandons the dark matter hypothesis entirely, accounting for anomalous galactic rotation curves by introducing a universal acceleration constant ($a_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m s}^{-2}$) below which Newtonian gravity breaks down. MOND's striking empirical success, particularly in explaining the Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR) and the Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation (BTFR)\citep{Lelli2017}, demonstrates that galactic kinematics are deeply coupled to the distribution of baryonic matter. 

\subsection{An alternative: Gas Based Models}
Sancisi and others have pointed out the puzzling connection of gas density to the rotation curves. In these gas models, gas is scaled by a factor of about  7\citep{hoekstraApparentCouplingNeutral2001}. The Hoekstra method of simply multiplying the gas mass by a number  doesn't work in general, and as more and better data came along, such as the SPARC dataset, it was put aside.

\subsection{Our Model} \label{subsec:ourmodel}

The density of dark matter is obtained by this relation, where $k$ is scaling constant near one. 
\begin{equation} \label{mainEquation}
    \rho_{\text{dm}} =k\  m_e \alpha^2 / a_B d^2.
    \qquad
\end{equation}
$d$ is the mean particle spacing in normal matter, $a_B$ is the Bohr radius, $\alpha$ is the fine structure constant, and $m_e$ the electron mass.

The $1/d^2$ can also be written as  $n^{2/3}$, where $n$ is the number density of matter particles. Thus, as a gas becomes more rarefied, the amount of dark matter 'generated' by our model as compared to the density of the particles increases. It in fact increases without limit in equation (\ref{mainEquation}). This is an issue for intergalactic levels of particle densities, so for densities less than $n_{cut} = 10^{-5}/cc$ we turn off the effect with an exponential switch. This effect cutoff has no effect on galactic rotation curve fitting, but is used to turn off our model at the halo - intergalactic boundary near $R_{200}$. The physical justification for this (if there is one), is that $n_{cut}$ is about where gas particles stop behaving like a gas and are instead ballistic.  

With $k \approx	1$, a gas with a particle number density of $10^{-5}cm^{-3}$ would have a dark matter density of about 150 times that of the gas itself, while at $100 cm^{-3}$ our equation (\ref{mainEquation}) only adds about $50\%$ to the density of the gas. (\ref{mainEquation}) might even hold in the everyday matter around us, for example for water, this formula would add about 0.001g to 0.1g to a ton of water. We assume that this dark mass has a negligible effect on individual particle dynamics. 

During the investigation, we determined that many SPARC galaxies have 'HI holes' near the core, which is a physically real phenomenon. There is more gas in galaxies than the HI gas listed in the SPARC database. 

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{CGM gas}: Not in SPARC: our model makes it contribute some mass even inside $R_{max}$. Outside $R_{max}$ the CGM gas combined with equation (\ref{mainEquation}) constructs a dark matter halo. These halos thus have a reasonable $R_{200}$ mass.  
	\item \textbf{H2/Molecular}: Included in SPARC, but hidden in the stellar masses. We require particle densities to make equation (\ref{mainEquation}) work. 
	\item \textbf{Others}: Stellar winds, star forming regions, galactic precipitation, etc. 
	\item \textbf{Electrons}: equation (\ref{mainEquation}) has no reference to baryons, ionized gas should therefore count twice. We consider this effect, which makes the particles we add have a mass of about 0.6 proton masses on average. This means that most of the gas we are adding is ionized. This might be a bit aggressive. COnsider though that by volume, the centres of galaxies are mostly ionized gas, and that the unionized gas mass is included in the $\Upsilon_{*}$ ratio in the core region. The CGM is also ionized, especially by volume.   
\end{itemize}

The extent and mass of some of these particle densities is unknown in detail. Most fits of our model add gas in to replace this hidden gas.
 
The SPARC dataset only includes HI gas measurements. There are other sources of gas in a galaxy. In addition, the centres of many galaxies are opaque to some gas measurements\citep{saintongeColdInterstellarMedium2022}, so the gas composition and densities at the cores of galaxies are not well known, especially for the purposes of our model, where hidden gas in the galactic core cannot be subsumed into a minor dynamical change in the stellar luminosity parameter, $\Upsilon_{*}$ \citep{lelliSPARCMassModels2016}.

\section{Methodology} \label{sec:meth}
In this paper, references to 'Lelli' are referring to the \textit{Lelli, McGaugh, and Schombert - SPARC: Mass Models for 175 Disk Galaxies with Spitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves.} \citep{lelliSPARCMassModels2016}. When we refer to Lelli, we are using unmodified data, etc from that paper. 

We perform 5 runs of our model, each run is over all 175 galaxies.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \label{Newton}\textbf{Newton}: A check on our model. 	
	\item \label{bestk}\textbf{Best k}: A fit to determine the best scaling factor $k$, using only the gas in SPARC dataset.
	\item \label{coregasglobal}\textbf{Core Gas Global}: a single global parameter is used to add  gas to each galaxy. (one parameter)
	\item \label{coregasindividual}\textbf{Core Gas Individual}: each galaxy is fitted with one parameter, the amount of gas to add. (175 parameters)
	\item \label{cgmrun}\textbf{CGM}: Both core gas fits have a circumgalactic medium (CGM) gas added, which models the dark matter halo.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Stellar Mass Luminosity Ratio}
The Stellar Mass Luminosity Ratio($\Upsilon$ or $M_\odot/L_\odot$) is the ratio of the total mass of a stellar population (or galaxy) to its total luminosity.
Following \citet{lelliSPARCMassModels2016}, we adopt $\Upsilon_\mathrm{disk}=0.5$ and $\Upsilon_\mathrm{bul}=0.7$. The SPARC gas term includes only H\,\textsc{i} (scaled by 1.33 for helium); molecular gas is neglected, and where it follows the stars it is implicitly part of $\Upsilon_\star$. This matters most in the inner regions of bulge galaxies, where H$_2$ often replaces a central H\,\textsc{i} depression, so some of the mass attributed to the bulge there may be gas, which our added core gas models. 

\subsubsection{SPARC gas density}
Since our model requires a gas density, and the SPARC dataset only has a gas velocity, a small program was written to determine plausible densities, given the $V_{gas}$ data in SPARC. The problem is under-defined. We required the gas distribution to be as smooth as possible while still recreating the $V_{gas}$ from SPARC (which itself was created by analysis of gas density measurements). In run 0 we plotted this gas as a velocity for each galaxy to verify that our calculated gas densities were reasonable. 


\subsection{General outline of the fits}
One of the main goals was to deviate from the parameters and methods in Lelli only when required. 
\subsubsection{3D volumetric approach}
We determine the acceleration due to gravity at each $R_{obs}$ radius of the SPARC dataset from all baryons with the addition of dark mass from our model. The chosen method uses a 3D volumetric approach where each galaxy is modelled in a cylindrical system with a few million cells per galaxy. Note that since the velocity contributions from stars are not modified, the 3D volume model is only used to store particle densities in each cell. Each cell then has an X, Y, Z position, a density of gas particles, and a volume. The gravitational attraction for each of the radial points in the SPARC dataset is determined by summing the millions of individual cell masses at their distance from each radial test point. This method thus covers induced acceleration from matter at radii beyond the radial measuring point. Each galaxy is modelled as a disk with thickness as calculated using the same formula as the SPARC 2016\citep{lelliSPARCMassModels2016}: an exponential vertical distribution with scale half height $z_d = 0.196 R_d^{0.633}$. The bulge is ignored for the reconstructed SPARC gas. Any gas added beyond our SPARC reconstruction is done in a spherically symmetric way. The 3D model is large enough (1.1 times $R_{max}$) so that any gas—whether in or outside of the disk—will contribute to acceleration and mass.

\subsection{Error and $\chi^2$ fitting}
The SPARC data include errors in the observed rotation velocities, but the errors in the other parameters are unknown and uncertain. For instance errors in inclination directly affect the reported $V_{obs}$, and gas density errors effect the $V_{gas}$, etc. In \citep{liFittingRadialAcceleration2018} , the authors fit the 175 SPARC galaxies individually, with up to 4 free parameters per galaxy. Many of the best fit parameters such as inclination are a factor of 2 or more away from accepted values. This is an indication of the uncertainty in the physical layout of our galactic neighbours. 

To capture this uncertainty, we assume a $15\%$ error in the calculated $V_{tot}$. If an error is not assumed fitting algorithims will pour their effort into minimizing the error on a few of the galaxies that have a large number of highly accurate $V_{obs}$. Our model also magnifies uncertainties - the dark mass contribution in our model has a higher uncertainty. Changing the assumed error by doubling it or halving it only made marginal changes to the derived fit parameters. \citep{schombertStellarMasstolightRatios2022} quote a 10\% error on gas components.  

In addition to this $\chi^2$ fitting algorithm, other fitting measures were tested, but the overall results only changed best fit outputs by a small amount. 

Error results are shown as per point only, as the the measurements are highly correlated. Due to the exploratory nature of this work, a full Bayesian analysis is outside the scope.

\subsection{Core Gas Global}
In order to add a gas core to every galaxy in the dataset, we needed to scale the added gas to the mass and/or size of the galaxy. The additional gas is added
via this formula:
\begin{equation}
    n_{\text{core}}(r) = n_0 \left[ 1 + \left( \frac{r}{f\,r_s} \right)^2 \right]^{-\beta}.
    \qquad
\end{equation}

Where $n_{\text{core}}(r)$ is the added density of gas at the spherical radius r. $n_0$ is the central
particle density, $\beta = 2.25$, $f = 2.5$, and
$M_{\text{gal}} = \left( L_{[3.6]}/L_\odot + M_{\text{HI}}/M_\odot \right) M_\odot$ and: 
\begin{equation}
    r_s = 2\,\text{kpc} \left( \frac{M_{\text{gal}}}{10^{11}\,M_\odot} \right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}

Note: $L_{[3.6]}$ and $M_{\text{HI}}$ are directly from SPARC database columns.

The only free parameter is $n_0$. In this fit, a single best $n_0$ was found by minimizing the error while at the same time optimizing the scaling constant, $k$ to be near 1.

\subsection{The MOND fit}

The MOND fit in this work is a very simple implementation: Using the MOND formula in the form given by \citep{Lelli2017} with Lelli gas only:

\begin{equation}
    g_{\text{bar}} = \frac{V_{\text{disk}}^2 + V_{\text{bul}}^2 + V_{\text{gas}}|V_{\text{gas}}|}{R},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    g_{\text{MOND}} = \frac{g_{\text{bar}}}{1 - e^{-\sqrt{g_{\text{bar}} / g_{\dagger}}}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{MOND}} = \sqrt{g_{\text{MOND}}\,R},
    \qquad
    g_{\dagger} = 1.2 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m\,s}^{-2}.
\end{equation}
Result:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\chi^2_{MOND} = 50 $ per point.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Universal Scaling Model}
The local dark matter density ($\rho_{\text{dm}}$) is assumed to follow a geometric power-law relation:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\text{dm}} = P/c^3 \cdot n_b^{2/3},
\end{equation}
where $n_b$ represents the local volumetric baryon density (calculated here from the raw SPARC neutral hydrogen gas profiles), and $P$ is a universal invariant constant across all systems, determined by a best fit to the SPARC data.  Our best fit of the parameter $P$ is: 

\begin{equation}
P \approx 59\ Watts.
\end{equation}


\subsection{Statistical Optimization via Least Absolute Deviations (LAD)}
Because galactic rotation curve data points exhibit localized spatial dependencies (e.g., radial proximity within individual galaxies sharing systematic streaming motions or beam smearing), traditional ordinary least squares ($\chi^2$) approaches can be disproportionately skewed by local outliers. We instead evaluate the goodness-of-fit using the Least Absolute Deviations (LAD) framework. 

The global model parameters are optimized by directly minimizing the total absolute residual sum of the observed ($V_{\text{obs}}$) and predicted ($V_{\text{pred}}$) rotation velocities across the entire ensemble of $N = 3,391$ data points:
\begin{equation}
\text{Total LAD} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} |V_{\text{obs}, i} - V_{\text{pred}, i}|
\end{equation}
The resulting model fit is characterized via the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), defined as $\text{MAE} = \text{Total LAD} / N$, providing a, velocity-space measure of global model precision in units of km/s.

\subsection{The interpretation of Power}
Power is about 10W. If a single baryon were literally emitting or processing 10 Watts of power, it would burn through its own rest mass in a fraction of a nanosecond.
Therefore, $P$ cannot be a literal power output of an individual particle. It could be a steady state relationship (as it relies on neighboring particles.  Or, Power might represent a coupling constant of baryons to the vacuum or spacetime itself. It defines a linear mass density scale ($P/c^3 \approx 10^{-24} \text{ kg/m}$) that dictates how baryonic mass curves space beyond its Newtonian boundary.

\subsection{Model Results}
The global optimization of our one-parameter model across all SPARC observations, results in a MAE of 26~km/s. The MOND framework as implemented here scores ($\text{MAE} = 16\text{ km/s}$). Our model achieves a remarkably similar accuracy profile using only a single universal parameter, like MOND. But unlike MOND, our model does not require the local particles to 'know' the global acceleration parameter, rather, they just have to 'know' the density of the material they are in. 

\subsection{Model Variations}
We also ran the model by adding 175 more fitting parameters, allowing each galaxy fit to adjust the entire gas density up or down by up to a factor of 5. This resulted in an ($\text{MAE} = 17\text{ km/s}$). 

In several SPARC radial bins, central core gas densities are recorded as zero. To test sensitivity to this, we implemented a physically motivated observational detection floor of $0.45\text{ baryons/cm}^3$ for unrecorded or low gas densities. Enforcing this floor shifts the global MAE by a negligible fraction, but it does make the global fit line up with Newton extremely well. 

These two model variations were not used in any of the other discussions in this paper, full results for them and a few others are available online.

\subsection{Core Dynamics and Distribution Tails}
An analysis of the absolute residual distributions reveals that MOND's localized advantage is concentrated almost entirely in a tight low-error spike between 0 and 15 km/s, corresponding to high-density inner galactic cores. This divergence is physically expected; our model evaluates dark mass generation based purely on the gas density profiles, which frequently drop or fluctuate in stellar-bulge dominated cores. Crucially, in the intermediate and long-range tails ($25\text{ to }100\text{ km/s}$) - representing the low-acceleration, outer-disk environments - the error distributions of our model and MOND track one another identically. This strongly implies that in the highly rarefied outer halo regime, our geometric density relation functions as a direct mathematical dual to MOND's physical behaviour, without requiring complex acceleration interpolations.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we have evaluated a universal, one-parameter empirical dark matter density profile defined by the geometric relation $\rho_{\text{dm}} = P/c^3 \cdot n_b^{2/3}$ against the complete SPARC database. By optimizing the global constant via robust Least Absolute Deviations (LAD) regression across 3,391 data points from 175 morphologically diverse galaxies, our model achieves a global Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 26~km/s. This performance is comparable to the MOND framework ($\text{MAE} = 16\text{ km/s}$). Both frameworks use a single parameter to model DM across all galaxies.

The statistical parsimony of this model provides compelling evidence for a universal coupling between baryonic density and dark mass distribution. We demonstrate that the $2/3$ scaling law captures the observations of the dataset.  

An analysis of the absolute residual distributions reveals that while MOND maintains an advantage in dense, stellar-dominated galactic cores, the two models track one another identically across the low-acceleration, outer-disk environments. This suggests that in the highly rarefied, widely spaced gas structures of the outer halo, our geometric density relation functions as a direct mathematical dual to MOND's phenomenological behaviour.

By focusing strictly on a data-driven, observational approach, this work side-steps the unconstrained parameter space of particle physics models that have remained undetected across decades of microphysical searches. We do not propose a specific microphysical mechanism for the $2/3$ scaling law, nor do we speculate on the fundamental properties of a constituent particle. Instead, we present this universal relation as an empirical reality mandated by galactic kinematics. We leave it to the particle physics and theoretical communities to engineer a fundamental mechanism---whether rooted in environmental screening, field-baryon interactions, or quantum vacuum geometries---capable of reproducing the strict observational constraints demonstrated here.
